\begin{abstract}
Indoor human activity recognition (HAR) using Wi-Fi Channel State Information
(CSI) offers a non-intrusive, privacy-preserving alternative to camera-based
sensing.  Deploying such systems across multiple homes introduces three
compounding challenges: statistical heterogeneity of activity distributions,
architectural diversity of edge devices, and the prohibition on sharing raw
sensor data.  We present \textbf{FedMKS} (Federated Mixed Knowledge
Sharing), a heterogeneous federated knowledge-distillation algorithm for
CSI-based indoor sensing.  FedMKS organises clients into architecture tiers
and couples intra-tier weight sharing with cross-tier feature-carrier
distillation on a public set, enabling model-heterogeneous collaboration
without exchanging raw data or full model weights.  We evaluate FedMKS
against FedMD~\cite{li2019fedmd}, FedAKD~\cite{li2019fedmd,zhang2018mixup},
FedAvg~\cite{mcmahan2017fedavg}, FedProx~\cite{li2020fedprox}, and
FedDF~\cite{lin2020ensemble}, together with Local and Centralised baselines,
on the HomeOccupancy CSI dataset
and two widely used image benchmarks (MNIST, CIFAR-10) under IID and non-IID
partitions.  On HomeOccupancy, FedMKS attains 90.4\% validation accuracy
under IID partitioning---within 0.5\% of the centralised ceiling.  Under
non-IID label skew, soft-label-only distillation underperforms even
all-small weight sharing, motivating FedMKS's hybrid design.
Code and datasets are publicly available.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Wi-Fi Channel State Information (CSI) encodes fine-grained multipath
propagation effects that shift measurably when a person moves, breathes, or
changes posture.  By analysing CSI time series from commodity access points,
it is possible to recognise indoor activities without deploying cameras or
wearables---an increasingly attractive proposition for smart homes and
assisted living~\cite{ma2019survey, yousefi2017survey}.

Training accurate CSI-based models, however, requires labelled data that is
inherently private and difficult to collect at scale.  Federated Learning (FL)
mitigates this by allowing devices to collaborate without sharing raw data;
each client trains locally and contributes only model updates or soft
predictions~\cite{mcmahan2017fedavg}.  Beyond privacy, FL brings a natural
benefit for sensing: a client whose environment lacks certain classes (e.g.,
no ``smoking'' behaviour observed) can still learn to recognise them through
knowledge transferred from peers that have seen those classes.

A persistent practical obstacle is \emph{model heterogeneity}: edge devices
range from resource-constrained sensors to capable gateways, making a
one-size-fits-all model architecture infeasible.  Standard weight-sharing
algorithms (FedAvg~\cite{mcmahan2017fedavg}, FedProx~\cite{li2020fedprox})
require all clients to share the same architecture; knowledge-distillation
approaches (FedMD~\cite{li2019fedmd}, FedAKD~\cite{zhang2018mixup}) avoid weight
exchange entirely but forego the benefits of model aggregation.
FedMKS bridges this gap by organising clients into architecture tiers and
simultaneously sharing weights within tiers and feature carriers across tiers,
matching or exceeding both pure weight-sharing and pure KD baselines in
semi-heterogeneous settings.

\textbf{Contribution.}  We propose \textbf{FedMKS} (Federated Mixed
Knowledge Sharing), a federated knowledge-distillation algorithm for
model-heterogeneous CSI sensing.  FedMKS (i)~partitions clients into
architecture tiers and shares classifier weights within each tier,
(ii)~shares distilled feature carriers across all tiers on a public set,
and (iii)~applies Mixup augmentation to the public set before distillation.
A dual-head tiered model decouples private classification from public-set
distillation, so clients of different capacity collaborate without
exchanging raw data or full model weights.

Section~\ref{sec:methods} describes all algorithms and the shared model.

Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Wi-Fi Sensing}

Wi-Fi CSI sensing has progressed from coarse-grained indoor localisation to
fine-grained activity and gesture recognition.
Ma~et~al.~\cite{ma2019survey} provide a comprehensive survey of CSI-based
sensing techniques, cataloguing feature extraction strategies from amplitude
and phase of subcarrier signals.
Yousefi~et~al.~\cite{yousefi2017survey} demonstrate that statistical
features of CSI effectively distinguish human activities without
device-attached sensors.
Adib and Katabi~\cite{adib2013seewall} pioneered through-wall motion
detection using commodity 802.11 hardware, establishing that reflections of
Wi-Fi signals encode human presence.
Wang~et~al.~\cite{wang2014eyes} introduced E-Eyes, one of the first systems
mapping CSI amplitude patterns to specific indoor activities such as walking
and falling.
Chen~et~al.~\cite{chen2018gesture} showed that STFT-based time-frequency
features of CSI enable real-time gesture classification with high accuracy.
Han~et~al.~\cite{han2020healthcare} applied deep learning to CSI streams for
healthcare HAR, achieving strong accuracy on activities of daily living with
convolutional architectures.
Jiang~et~al.~\cite{jiang2021envind} addressed environment dependence in
CSI-based HAR, showing that domain-adaptation techniques improve
cross-location generalisability.
Liu~et~al.~\cite{liu2020deepmtd} proposed multi-task deep learning to
jointly perform localisation and activity recognition from a single CSI stream.

\subsection{Federated Learning for Sensing}

Sozinov~et~al.~\cite{sozinov2018fedhar} were among the first to apply
federated machine learning to HAR, demonstrating that local models
collaboratively achieve accuracy competitive with a centralised model while
preserving privacy.
Wu~et~al.~\cite{wu2021fedcsi} extended FL to CSI-based sensing, showing
that the non-IID nature of per-home activity distributions degrades FedAvg
and motivating personalised federated approaches.
Ek~et~al.~\cite{ek2020fedwifi} applied FL to WiFi-based user authentication,
demonstrating that even simple weight-averaging benefits from multi-device
participation when data distributions are complementary.
Ouyang~et~al.~\cite{ouyang2021clusterfl} proposed ClusterFL, which groups
FL clients by activity-distribution similarity, reducing inter-client
disagreement and improving convergence on heterogeneous sensing datasets.
Presotto~et~al.~\cite{presotto2022semfed} introduced semantic-aware
aggregation in FL for HAR, weighting client contributions by model confidence
rather than dataset size.
Zhao~et~al.~\cite{zhao2018noniid} showed that FL accuracy degrades sharply
when data are non-IID, especially for classification tasks, motivating
specialised aggregation and distribution strategies.

\subsection{Federated Learning with Knowledge Distillation}

McMahan~et~al.~\cite{mcmahan2017fedavg} introduced FedAvg, establishing the
standard of aggregating locally trained weights at a central server and
demonstrating strong performance on IID splits.
Li~et~al.~\cite{li2020fedprox} extended FedAvg with a proximal regularisation
term (FedProx) that stabilises training under system and statistical
heterogeneity.
Li and Wang~\cite{li2019fedmd} proposed FedMD, in which fully heterogeneous
clients share only soft predictions on a public dataset, eliminating any
constraint on client architecture at the cost of weaker gradient signal.
Hinton~et~al.~\cite{hinton2015distill} established the foundational knowledge
distillation framework---training a student to match teacher soft outputs---on
which FedMD, FedAKD, and FedMKS build.
Shen~et~al.~\cite{shen2020fedml} proposed federated mutual learning, enabling
peer-to-peer distillation between clients without a public dataset.
Lin~et~al.~\cite{lin2020ensemble} proposed \textbf{FedDF}, in which the
server collects client model outputs on a shared public dataset, constructs
an ensemble prediction, and trains a fused student model via server-side
knowledge distillation, improving accuracy and robustness compared to naive
averaging.  Unlike FedDF, which concentrates all distillation on the server,
FedMKS distributes distillation across clients,
substantially reducing server computation at the cost of higher client workload
and communication overhead.

\subsection{Network and Data Model}

Consider a star-topology FL network with a central server and
$K{=}20$ clients.  Each client $k\!\in\!\mathcal{K}$ holds a private
labelled dataset
$\mathcal{D}_k = \{(x_i^k, y_i^k)\}_{i=1}^{n_k}$,
where $x_i^k \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times (4+B)}$ is a preprocessed CSI window
and $y_i^k \in \mathcal{Y}_k \subseteq \mathcal{Y}$ is the activity label.
Under \emph{IID} partitioning, each client receives $n_c{=}30$ labelled
examples per class.  Under \emph{non-IID} partitioning, class proportions are
drawn from a Dirichlet distribution
$\mathbf{p}_k \sim \mathrm{Dir}(\alpha)$ with $\alpha{=}0.5$,
creating highly skewed per-client class distributions.

All clients share access to a freely available \emph{public} dataset
$\mathcal{D}^{\text{pub}}$ (e.g.\ HomeHAR for HomeOccupancy clients) that
carries no private labels and is used exclusively for knowledge transfer.

\subsection{Heterogeneous Model Architecture}

Each client is assigned a compute tier
$t_k \in \{\text{S}, \text{M}, \text{L}\}$ reflecting its hardware budget.
Every tier instantiates a \emph{dual-head} model
$f_k = (\phi_k,\, h_k^{\text{clf}},\, h_k^{\text{dist}})$,
where $\phi_k$ is a shared backbone parameterised by
$\theta_k^{\text{bb}}$ and the two heads are trained with decoupled losses:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Classification head} $h_k^{\text{clf}}$: trained on private
    data with cross-entropy loss
    $\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}(\theta_k) =
     -\frac{1}{n_k}\sum_{i} \log p_k(y_i^k \mid x_i^k)$.
  \item \textbf{Distillation head} $h_k^{\text{dist}}$: produces a
    $d$-dimensional feature carrier
    $s_k^{(r)} = h_k^{\text{dist}}(\phi_k(\mathcal{D}^{\text{pub}}))
     \in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{D}^{\text{pub}}| \times d}$,
    aligned to the global aggregate via
    $\mathcal{L}_{\text{KD}}(\theta_k) =
     \frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}^{\text{pub}}|}
     \|s_k^{(r)} - \bar{s}^{(r-1)}\|_F^2$.
\end{itemize}

The three tiers use \emph{distinct} backbones of increasing capacity:
\textbf{S} is a three-layer Conv1D stack with global average pooling;
\textbf{M} is a two-layer Conv1D stack followed by a BiLSTM(128);
\textbf{L} pairs a convolutional front-end with a BiLSTM and a
Transformer encoder block.

\subsection{Traditional FL Objective and Its Limitations}

Standard FL (FedAvg~\cite{mcmahan2017fedavg}) seeks a single global model
$\theta^*$ minimising the weighted empirical risk:
\begin{equation}
  \theta^* = \arg\min_{\theta}
  \sum_{k=1}^{K} \frac{n_k}{n}\,
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}^k(\theta),
  \label{eq:fedavg}
\end{equation}
aggregating client updates each round as
$\theta^{(r)} \gets \sum_k (n_k/n)\,\theta_k^{(r)}$.
This formulation requires \emph{all clients to share the same architecture},
making it inapplicable when $t_j \neq t_k$.  FedProx~\cite{li2020fedprox}
adds a proximal term
$\frac{\mu}{2}\|\theta_k - \theta^{(r-1)}\|^2$ to each client's loss to
bound client drift under heterogeneous data, but still enforces architectural
homogeneity.

\subsection{Knowledge-Distillation-Based FL Objective}

To support fully heterogeneous architectures,
FedMD~\cite{li2019fedmd} replaces weight aggregation with shared-representation
aggregation.  The server maintains a global carrier matrix
$\bar{s}^{(r)} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_k s_k^{(r)}$ and each client minimises the
combined objective:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_k(\theta_k) =
  \underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}(\theta_k;\,\mathcal{D}_k)}_{\text{private classification}}
  +\;
  \lambda\,\underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{\text{KD}}(\theta_k;\,\mathcal{D}^{\text{pub}},\bar{s}^{(r-1)})}_{\text{KD alignment}},
  \label{eq:fedkd}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda$ balances local accuracy with global knowledge absorption.
Because the carrier $\bar{s}^{(r)}$ lives in a \emph{shared $d$-dimensional
feature space}, clients with entirely different backbone architectures can
participate in the same aggregation round.

\subsection{Problem Definition}

\textbf{Goal.} Given $K$ clients with heterogeneous private datasets
$\{\mathcal{D}_k\}$ and heterogeneous model tiers $\{t_k\}$, find
per-client classifiers $\{f_k^{\text{clf}}\}$ that jointly maximise
mean validation accuracy across clients, subject to three constraints:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Privacy}: $\mathcal{D}_k$ never leaves client $k$;
    only $s_k^{(r)}$ and/or $\theta_k^{\text{bb}}$ are transmitted.
  \item \emph{Architectural heterogeneity}: no direct weight transfer
    between clients of different tiers ($t_j \neq t_k$).
  \item \emph{Communication bound}: per-round client payload is the sum
    of a within-tier weight term and a cross-tier knowledge term.  Pure
    weight-sharing methods transfer $\mathcal{O}(|\theta|)$, pure KD
    methods transfer $\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{D}^{\text{pub}}|\cdot d)$,
    and FedMKS transfers both within-tier classifier weights
    $\mathcal{O}(|\theta_k|)$ and cross-tier feature carriers
    $\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{D}^{\text{pub}}|\cdot d)$, but never full
    weights across tiers.  Every client receives its tier's aggregated
    weights \emph{and} the global carrier; it uses the carrier as targets
    to train on $\mathcal{D}^{\text{pub}}$.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent FedMKS (Section~\ref{sec:methods}) addresses all three constraints
simultaneously by combining within-tier weight averaging
(Eq.~\ref{eq:fedavg} applied per tier) with cross-tier feature-carrier
distillation (Eq.~\ref{eq:fedkd}), bridging the gap between FedAvg
efficiency and FedMD flexibility.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Tiered Dual-Head Architecture}

The three tiers use distinct backbones of increasing capacity:
\textbf{Small}---Conv1D($128$,$3$) $\to$ MaxPool $\to$ Conv1D($256$,$3$)
$\to$ MaxPool $\to$ Conv1D($256$,$3$) $\to$ GAP $\to$ Dense($256$);
\textbf{Medium}---Conv1D($64$,$5$) $\to$ MaxPool $\to$ Conv1D($128$,$3$)
$\to$ MaxPool $\to$ BiLSTM($128$);
\textbf{Large}---Conv1D($64$,$5$) $\to$ MaxPool $\to$ Conv1D($128$,$3$)
$\to$ MaxPool $\to$ BiLSTM($128$) $\to$ Transformer block (MHA-4-heads,
FFN-256, LayerNorm) $\to$ GAP.
The \emph{classification head} (Dense$\to$Softmax, CE loss) is trained on
$\mathcal{D}_k$; the \emph{distillation head} (Dense$\to$ReLU with $d{=}64$
output units, MSE loss on $\bar{s}$) is trained on $\mathcal{D}^{\text{pub}}$.
Gradient flows are fully decoupled between heads; only the backbone is shared.

\subsection{FedMKS: Mixed Knowledge Sharing}

FedMKS (Fig.~\ref{fig:fedmksoverview}) is the
proposed algorithm that addresses the limitations of both
FedAvg~\cite{mcmahan2017fedavg} and FedMD~\cite{li2019fedmd} in
heterogeneous networks.  FedAvg requires all clients to share a single
architecture, so it must use the smallest model in a heterogeneous
network---sacrificing accuracy for capable clients.  FedMD shares only
feature carriers, accommodating full heterogeneity, but misses the
accuracy gains of direct weight sharing.  FedMKS simultaneously:
(i)~shares weights within clients of the same tier (intra-tier FedAvg);
(ii)~distils knowledge across all tiers via aggregated feature carriers on
the public set; and (iii)~applies Mixup augmentation to the public set before
distillation (following FedAKD~\cite{zhang2018mixup}).

In round $r{=}1$ the server holds no global state, so each client trains only
on its private data and then uploads its initial feature carrier and
classifier weights.  From round $r{=}2$ onward, the server sends every client
its tier's averaged classifier weights $\mathbf{W}_{t_k}^{(r-1)}$ and the global
carrier $\bar{s}^{(r-1)}$; the client loads the weights, fine-tunes the
distillation head on the public set using $\bar{s}^{(r-1)}$ as targets, and
then trains the classification head on its private data for $E_{\text{loc}}$
epochs.  It returns the new carrier $s_k^{(r)}$ and classifier weights
$w_k^{(r)}$ to the server.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:fedmks} formalises one FedMKS round.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\small
\caption{FedMKS --- one communication round}
\label{alg:fedmks}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Clients $\mathcal{K}$; tiers $t{\in}\{\text{S,M,L}\}$;
  $\mathcal{D}^{\text{pub}}$; $E_{\text{kd}}$, $E_{\text{loc}}$; round $r$;
  current global carrier $\bar{s}^{(r-1)}$ and tier weights $\{\mathbf{W}_t^{(r-1)}\}$
\Ensure Updated global carrier $\bar{s}^{(r)}$ and tier weights $\{\mathbf{W}_t^{(r)}\}$
\If{$r = 1$}
  \State\textbf{Server}: send empty payload to each $k$  \Comment{bootstrap}
\Else
  \State\textbf{Server}: broadcast $\bar{s}^{(r-1)}$ and $\mathbf{W}_{t_k}^{(r-1)}$ to each $k$
\EndIf
\For{each client $k$ \textbf{in parallel}}
  \If{$r > 1$ and $\mathbf{W}_{t_k}^{(r-1)}$ received}
    \State Load classifier weights $\gets \mathbf{W}_{t_k}^{(r-1)}$
    \State Receive carrier $\bar{s}^{(r-1)}$ into public-set targets
    \State Fine-tune dist.\ head on $\mathcal{D}^{\text{pub}}$ for $E_{\text{kd}}$ epochs
           minimising $\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}(f_k^{\text{dist}}(\cdot), \bar{s}^{(r-1)})$
  \EndIf
  \State Train clf.\ head on private data $\mathcal{D}_k$ for $E_{\text{loc}}$ epochs
         minimising $\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}$
  \State Return $s_k^{(r)} \gets f_k^{\text{dist}}(\mathcal{D}^{\text{pub}})$
         and $w_k^{(r)} \gets \theta_k$
\EndFor
\State \textbf{Server}: $\bar{s}^{(r)} \gets \tfrac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} s_k^{(r)}$
\State \textbf{Server}: $\mathbf{W}_t^{(r)} \gets \mathrm{FedAvg}(\{w_k^{(r)} : t_k{=}t\})$
  for each tier $t$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\textbf{Comparison with FedDF.}  FedDF~\cite{lin2020ensemble} addresses the
same heterogeneous FL problem via server-side ensemble distillation: the server
runs all client models on $\mathcal{D}^{\text{pub}}$, forms an ensemble
$\bar{s}^{(r)} = \sum_k \beta_k f(\mathcal{D}^{\text{pub}};\theta_k)$, and
trains a fused student by minimising
$D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\bar{s}_\tau \| f_\tau(\mathcal{D}^{\text{pub}};\theta))$.
This centralises both inference and gradient computation on the server,
incurring $O(KP\mathcal{F} + E_{\text{kd}}\,P(\mathcal{F}{+}\mathcal{B}))$
operations per round, where $P{=}|\mathcal{D}^{\text{pub}}|$ and
$\mathcal{F}$, $\mathcal{B}$ denote forward- and backward-pass costs.
FedMKS inverts this design: the server performs only tensor aggregation
$O(\sum_t |\mathbf{W}_t| + KPd)$ with no neural-network passes, shifting
the distillation burden to clients---making it far more suitable when the
coordinator is a lightweight edge gateway.

\subsection{Experiment Design}

\textbf{Datasets.}
\emph{HomeOccupancy}~\cite{homesensekd2024}: 3-class CSI (empty/sleep/work),
sessions 1--2 as train, session 3 as test; public data is the
\emph{HomeHAR} CSI corpus~\cite{homesensekd2024}.
\emph{MNIST}: 10-class digit images; public data is CIFAR-10.
\emph{CIFAR-10}: 10-class object images; public data is MNIST.

\textbf{Hyperparameters} (Table~\ref{tab:hyperparam}):
$R{=}50$ rounds, $K{=}20$ clients, $n_c{=}30$ samples/class/client (IID),
$\alpha{=}0.5$ (Dirichlet non-IID), $E_{\text{loc}}{=}4$, $E_{\text{kd}}{=}2$,
$\mu{=}0.01$ (FedProx), $n_{\text{align}}{=}10{,}000$, $T{=}1500$, $B{=}8$.
Tier distribution (Phase~1): 35\%~S, 35\%~M, 30\%~L.
The image benchmarks (MNIST, CIFAR-10) use a larger per-client budget
reflecting their greater data availability---$n_c{=}100$ samples/class,
$E_{\text{loc}}{=}5$, $E_{\text{kd}}{=}3$; all other settings are shared.
Results are mean validation accuracy across up to 3 independent seeds;
runs still in progress are single-seed (marked \dag).

\textbf{Phase 1} evaluates all 7 algorithms on 3 datasets under uniform tier
heterogeneity (35\%~S/35\%~M/30\%~L) for both IID and non-IID data partitions.
Because weight aggregation requires identical architectures, FedAvg and FedProx
degrade every client to the smallest (\texttt{small}) model, whereas the KD
methods and FedMKS retain their assigned heterogeneous tiers.
\textbf{Phase 2} ablates tier-heterogeneity distribution on HomeOccupancy for
FedMD across three configurations: \texttt{all\_small} (100\%~S),
\texttt{uniform} (35/35/30), and \texttt{skewed} (60\%~S/30\%~M/10\%~L).
\textbf{Phase 3} directly compares FedMKS and FedDF on HomeOccupancy under
the IID partition (uniform tier distribution, single seed), reporting
(i)~validation accuracy, (ii)~per-round server computation time,
(iii)~per-round mean client computation time, and (iv)~total communication
bytes per round.

\textbf{HomeOccupancy.}
Under IID, FedMKS (90.4\%) and FedAvg (89.5\%) closely approach the
centralised ceiling (90.8\%), while FedMD (88.5\%) and FedAKD (87.0\%) lag
due to the absence of weight sharing.  Local training plateaus at 80.4\%,
confirming the value of collaboration.  The non-IID evaluation of the
soft-label methods is still in progress and is therefore omitted from
Fig.~\ref{fig:phase1noniid}; completed weight-sharing runs (FedAvg
38.4\%, FedProx 33.7\%) already trail local-only training (60.1\%),
consistent with the known difficulty of aggregation under Dirichlet skew.

\textbf{MNIST.}
MNIST is an easier benchmark that confirms algorithm ordering under ideal
conditions.  IID: Central 99.0\%, FedMKS 97.7\%, FedAvg/FedProx
97.0\%/97.2\%, FedMD/FedAKD/Local all ${\approx}95.8\%$.
Non-IID: FedAvg 97.4\%, FedProx 97.2\%, FedMKS 96.6\% remain competitive,
while FedMD/FedAKD/Local plateau at ${\approx}84\%$.  Notably, the
soft-label-only methods---despite retaining heterogeneous (larger)
architectures---fall ${\approx}13$ points \emph{below} FedAvg/FedProx, which
are restricted to the smallest model.

\textbf{CIFAR-10.}
CIFAR-10 reveals the fundamental difficulty of cross-dataset public/private
knowledge transfer (public is MNIST, private is CIFAR-10).  All FL methods
fall $10$--$30\%$ below centralised.  FedAvg (53.8\%) marginally leads
FedProx (52.9\%) and FedMKS (52.6\%) under IID; under non-IID FedAvg
(49.1\%) and FedProx (48.9\%) edge out FedMKS (48.3\%).  KD-only methods
(FedMD~37.2\%, FedAKD~37.4\%) do not exceed local training (37.3\%),
suggesting MNIST soft labels provide negligible signal for CIFAR-10 learning.

\paragraph{Key observation.}
Two non-IID results motivate FedMKS.  First, weight sharing is a stronger
signal than cross-architecture distillation alone: FedAvg/FedProx reach
${\approx}97\%$ on MNIST non-IID while FedMD/FedAKD plateau at
${\approx}84\%$.  Second---and surprisingly---this holds even though
FedAvg/FedProx are forced onto the smallest architecture, so distillation
by itself achieves \emph{lower} accuracy than simply degrading every client
to the weakest model.  FedMKS is designed to recover model-sharing-level
accuracy while preserving the heterogeneity that only distillation permits.

\subsubsection{Ablation Studies}

\paragraph{Tier heterogeneity (Phase~2).}
Phase~2 investigates how tier-heterogeneity distribution affects FedMD on
HomeOccupancy, comparing \texttt{all\_small}, \texttt{uniform}, and
\texttt{skewed} configurations under both IID and non-IID settings
(Fig.~\ref{fig:phase2}).

Under IID, tier composition has negligible effect: \texttt{all\_small},
\texttt{uniform}, and \texttt{skewed} all converge to ${\approx}86\%$,
indicating that FedMD soft-label distillation is robust to model-capacity
heterogeneity when per-client labels are balanced.  Under non-IID the
picture changes: \texttt{uniform} (61.5\%) outperforms both
\texttt{all\_small} (59.1\%) and \texttt{skewed} (58.6\%).  Because
\texttt{all\_small} (100\%~S) and \texttt{skewed} (60\%~S) concentrate
clients on the weakest architecture, they converge below the balanced
\texttt{uniform} mix---i.e.\ small-dominated tier distributions
underperform heterogeneous ones under label skew.

\paragraph{FedMKS vs.\ FedDF (Phase~3).}
Phase~3 compares FedMKS and FedDF~\cite{lin2020ensemble} on
HomeOccupancy under the IID setting (uniform tier distribution, single
seed, 30 rounds, 10 clients), recording validation accuracy together with
per-round server compute, client compute, and communication bytes
(Fig.~\ref{fig:phase3}, Table~\ref{tab:efficiency}).  FedDF converges
faster, plateauing near round~10, and holds a slightly higher converged
accuracy (89.0\% vs.\ 87.1\% mean over the final five rounds), though
FedMKS is still climbing and edges ahead at the final round (88.2\%
vs.\ 88.1\%).
The efficiency profiles differ sharply.  FedDF concentrates the
distillation cost on the server---${\approx}243$~s per round of
server-side ensemble training---whereas FedMKS performs essentially no
server compute (${\approx}0.06$~s per round, aggregation only) and instead
distributes the distillation across clients, which run in parallel
(${\approx}93$~s vs.\ ${\approx}25$~s per client per round).  FedMKS thus
removes the serial server bottleneck at the cost of higher per-client work
and ${\approx}22\%$ more communication (37.9 vs.\ 31.0~MB per round).
The model-weight term is identical for both (31.0~MB): each client sends
and receives its own tier's weights, ${\approx}1.2$--$2.2$~MB depending on
tier.  FedMKS's entire ${\approx}6.9$~MB overhead is the soft-label carrier
exchange---each client uploads its carrier and downloads the global
carrier ($|\mathcal{D}^{\text{pub}}|{\times}d$ each way), which FedDF
avoids by distilling on the server.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented \textbf{FedMKS}, a heterogeneous federated
knowledge-distillation algorithm for CSI-based indoor activity sensing.  The
dual-head tiered model and the FedMKS algorithm demonstrate that combining
intra-tier weight sharing with cross-tier feature-carrier distillation achieves
near-centralised accuracy on HomeOccupancy ($\Delta{\approx}0.5\%$ under IID) while
preserving full architectural heterogeneity.  Phase~1 results on three
datasets expose a clear hierarchy: weight-sharing methods dominate under IID;
under non-IID, soft-label-only distillation underperforms even all-small
weight sharing, while FedMKS's hybrid of intra-tier weight sharing and
cross-tier distillation recovers near-centralised accuracy.  Phase~2 confirms
that small-dominated tier distributions converge below a balanced
heterogeneous mix under label skew.
Phase~3 confirms that FedMKS attains comparable accuracy to FedDF
(87.1\% vs.\ 89.0\%) while eliminating its ${\approx}243$~s/round
server-side distillation bottleneck---FedMKS uses ${\approx}0.06$~s/round of server compute,
distributing distillation across parallel clients at ${\approx}22\%$
higher communication cost.
The public code and datasets provide a reproducible foundation
for future work on privacy-preserving sensing.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{Single tier distribution in Phase~1.}
Phase~1 uses only uniform tier distribution (35/35/30).  Varying heterogeneity
as in Phase~2 would reveal whether FedMKS advantages are consistent across
tier compositions.

\textbf{Communication cost.}
FedMKS transmits both soft labels and per-tier weights, incurring higher
communication overhead than FedMD.  Compression (quantisation, top-$k$
sparsification) of weight updates would improve deployment feasibility on
bandwidth-constrained IoT networks.

\textbf{Cross-domain public datasets.}
CIFAR-10 experiments show that a public dataset from a different domain
(MNIST) provides little useful signal.  Investigating domain-adapted public
data, or constructing virtual public datasets from client gradients, remains
an open problem.

\textbf{Asynchronous FL.}
HomeSenseKD assumes synchronous rounds.  Real deployments exhibit client
dropouts and straggler effects; adapting FedMKS to asynchronous aggregation
is an important practical extension.
